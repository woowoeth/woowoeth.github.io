The crossing

Julius Caesar (Rome · 100–44 BC) — The crossing.

Strategy and competitionQin, Han and the Six DynastiesThe crossing
Rome's most famous general and its dictator, and the source of the line about the die being cast. He went from an aristocrat buried in debt to the conquest of Gaul, victory in the civil war and dictator for life, in about twenty years. Two of his decisions are worth more than the biography: the pause at the Rubicon, which was arithmetic rather than nerve, and the policy of pardoning defeated enemies, which he explained in a letter and which eventually helped kill him.

What actually happened?

On the fifteenth of March, 44 BC he walked into the senate and was killed with twenty-three wounds. Among the men holding knives were Pompeians he had pardoned during the civil war and afterwards promoted, Brutus among them. He had waved away every warning that an attempt was coming, because he believed pardon bought loyalty. Suetonius reports that when he saw Brutus he spoke his last words in Greek: you too, my child? Everything he had built to defend himself faced outward, and nothing came from that direction.

The pause is the decision

He halted at the Rubicon a long while and said out loud that they could still turn back, but that past the little bridge it would all be settled by arms. Reversible decisions deserve speed; a decision that deletes the way back deserves the night.

Changing a tool is undone in a fortnight and needs no committee. An exclusive contract or a public side taken is worth a night at the bank.

The sums were done before the sentence

Ten years in Gaul had made his legions the best in Rome, Pompey's forces were scattered in Spain, and the city had nothing to hold it with. He took Italy in sixty days and almost without blood. The famous line is theatre placed on top of a calculation.

Before you commit in public, know your own core capability, the gap in theirs, and the position you can hold in the first month.

Mercy as a policy, not a mood

At Corfinium he released every captured senator and returned their property, and wrote to his staff that mercy and generosity would be their new way of winning, in explicit contrast to Sulla's terror. It worked because it changed what the other side expected defeat to mean.

When defeat means ruin, everyone fights to the end. When it means going home, resistance stops being worth paying for.

The narrative was part of the campaign

He wrote the Gallic War while campaigning and told it in the third person, so that the account reads as record rather than boast while building the name Caesar at the same time. Winning a war and owning the story of the war are separate pieces of work.

He did both. Most people who did the work do only the first, and then wonder why somebody else is credited with it.

What outlived him was the design

His adopted son used the name to build an empire, and the name itself became the word for emperor in languages he never heard, Kaiser and Tsar among them. What lasts is rarely the memorial; it is whether the thing you set up runs without you.

The test is unsentimental. Take yourself out for a quarter and see which of the things you built are still standing when you come back.

How do I use it today?

Sort your open decisions into two piles, the ones you can walk back and the ones you cannot. Move fast on the first pile immediately, and give one of the second pile a full evening with the numbers before you say anything out loud.
