The Old Regime and the Revolution

Improvement breeds discontent

The Old Regime and the Revolution (1856 · Alexis de Tocqueville) — Improvement breeds discontent.

How the world worksThe industrial ageImprovement breeds discontent
Tocqueville (1805–1859) came from the old French nobility and had family who went to the guillotine. He spent his life on one question: why did the Revolution happen in France of all places? The answer overturned everyone's common sense — it was not squeezed out by oppression, it was drawn out by improvement. The book appeared in 1856 and is still the sharpest account there is of why reform detonates a crisis.

What actually happened?

He wrote it after politics had thrown him out. Tocqueville had been foreign minister for five months in 1849; then Louis Napoleon seized power in 1851, and Tocqueville, who would not take the oath, was briefly arrested and finished with public life. He went to the archives at Tours instead — intendants' correspondence, tax rolls, parish complaints. His own parents had gone into prison under the Terror and come out only after Robespierre fell. The book is a man reading the file on the machine that nearly killed his family, and finding it still in service.

The Tocqueville paradox

People never judge their situation by its absolute level, but by the gap between how fast things improve and how fast expectation rises. Once improvement starts, expectation always climbs faster than reality, so the gap widens rather than closes. Nothing about human nature has degraded; the frame of reference was replaced.

Results recover, pay rises start, and attrition goes up — because the raise replaced 'this place is finished' with 'this place can give more', and the second always outruns the actual figure.

The abolished privilege is the one that stings

What people hate is never oppression as such; it is oppression that can no longer state its reason. While feudal obligation was a complete system it was simply how the world worked. Cut down to a few scattered items, the survivors lose their covering and become naked humiliation.

A company removes most management perks and keeps the executive parking spaces. The one that stays draws more resentment than the whole set used to.

Centralisation is the old regime's legacy, not the Revolution's invention

The most easily missed and most important chapter. From the archives Tocqueville shows that the Revolution, which advertised the destruction of the old regime, in fact inherited its core: a highly centralised administrative machine. It changed the operators, kept the machine, and made it stronger.

A team revolts against being micromanaged and gets rid of the old boss. Six months later the new one manages in more detail, because the reporting lines and the review system were never touched.

Writers in place of politicians

The old regime had detached the nobility from actual governing, leaving them privilege without responsibility, and handed public discussion to men of letters with no experience of office. France produced a politics in which the most thrilling abstractions came from the people least answerable for results.

In any organisation, once the people who propose and the people who bear the consequences are fully separated, proposals get more beautiful and less executable every round.

Where liberty and equality part

The anxiety of his life: the desire for equality is far stronger than the desire for liberty. For equality people will accept a stronger central power that levels everyone, themselves included. The end of that road is not tyranny returning but a mild supervision people walk into willingly.

The demand that everyone be the same very often ends in surrendering the right to decide for yourself, and flattening reorganisations are no exception.

How do I use it today?

Whenever you are about to start an improvement — a pay rise, devolved authority, a cleaner process, a redesigned product — do one thing first: work out which surviving unfairness it is about to push into the spotlight, and deal with that in the same move. Reform detonates a crisis almost never because too little was changed, but because A was changed and B was left where it was, so B became visible for the first time.
