Søren Kierkegaard (Denmark · 1813–1855) — Choosing.

Mind and feelingThe industrial ageChoosing
A Danish philosopher, born in Copenhagen in 1813 and dead in the same city in 1855. In 1843 he published Either/Or under a pseudonym, and that year also Fear and Trembling; in 1844 came The Concept of Anxiety. He signed different books with different invented names and let each voice argue its own position, without telling the reader which was right. What he wanted to know was not which answer is correct but how a person standing before two roads actually chooses, and answers for it.

What actually happened?

In October 1841, aged twenty-eight, Kierkegaard returned the engagement ring to Regine Olsen, a little over a year after they had become engaged. He never married. Two years later Either/Or appeared without his name on it: the editor was a made-up Victor Eremita, whose preface says he found the papers in a hidden drawer of an old writing desk.

The one choosing is you

Either/Or sets two ways of living side by side and does not announce a winner. The reader has to do the choosing, which is the point: no one else can do it in your place.

A friend lays out both options and says 'I would take the first', and you still have to be the one who signs.

Anxiety is the dizziness of freedom

In The Concept of Anxiety he says the dizziness comes from looking down into your own possibilities. The more you could do, the more it swims. He does not treat it as a fault; he treats it as what freedom feels like.

Standing in front of twelve career paths, you feel sick, and the sickness is a sign the choice is real.

Understood backwards, lived forwards

His journal says life can only be understood looking back, yet has to be lived looking ahead. So the clarity you are waiting for will not arrive before the decision; it arrives after.

Only on leaving a job do you see which of the early signs mattered.

Different names, one reader

He published under pseudonyms so that readers could not borrow his authority. If the voice is invented, you cannot say 'the author agrees with me'; you have to argue with the position itself.

A manager who writes the case against his own plan, and has someone else sign it.

How do I use it today?

Pick one small thing where you do not want to let either side go. Give yourself ten minutes, cross out the column that says 'afraid of regret', and write only which one you are more willing to answer for. Then do it, and do not reopen the other column.
