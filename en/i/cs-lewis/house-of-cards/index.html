What held you up your whole life didn't hold this time. What Lewis wrote when he found that out about himself.

What actually happened?

Lewis was among the most famous Christian writers of his day, the author of many books arguing people into belief. After his wife died he found that what he had been saying for decades didn't work on him. You never know how much you believe something, he wrote, until its truth or falsehood becomes a matter of life and death. He didn't rush to patch it. He admitted it: the house had been made of cards, and nobody had ever pushed it. What happened, he later said, was not that faith was taken away. It was tested for the first time.

A belief never tested may not be a belief

Everything he had argued for decades held in good weather and collapsed at the first real event. That doesn't prove the argument wrong; it proves it had never carried weight. Most of what people assume will hold them has never been loaded. The first loading is when you find out.

'I'm quite philosophical about these things' doesn't count as evidence until after something happens.

Don't rebuild immediately

He didn't find a new set of ideas to plug the hole. He let the hole sit for a while. People who rebuild in a hurry generally build the same house out of the same cards. Wait and see what is still standing afterwards — that is the actual foundation.

When the story that held you stops working, don't swap in another. Look at what's still upright.

'Tested' and 'taken away' are different things

At first he thought something had been taken from him. He later changed the word to tested. Same experience: the first phrasing makes you a victim, the second makes you someone finding out what's true. Only the second can be walked forward from.

'I lost it' and 'I found out for the first time how much of it there was' — take the second.

How do I use it today?

Where you are: the thing that always held you — a belief, a way of seeing things — didn't hold this time, and you feel you have nothing.

Ask first: had it ever carried weight before? What's still standing after the collapse? Can I say 'tested' instead of 'taken away'?

Where it goes wrong: reading it as 'everything anyone believes is false' — he didn't abandon his faith, he exchanged it for one not made of cards. This is about testing, not denial.

Lines to keep
