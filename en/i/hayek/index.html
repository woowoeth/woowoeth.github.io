Dispersed knowledge

Friedrich Hayek (Austria–Britain · 1899–1992) — Dispersed knowledge.

How the world worksThe industrial ageDispersed knowledge
The man who turned why planning goes systematically wrong into economics. Not because planners are lazy, but because the knowledge a plan needs sits in thousands of separate heads and will not travel. Prices are the transport protocol for that knowledge. He argued with Keynes for a lifetime, and what won in the end was not rhetoric but this one point about who can know what.

What actually happened?

By the 1950s he was, inside his own profession, close to forgotten. Keynes had won the argument, governments were managing demand, and Hayek had moved to Chicago into a committee on social thought rather than the economics department, which did not want him. He was seventy-five when the Nobel came in 1974, and he used the lecture to attack his own discipline: he called it The Pretence of Knowledge, and argued that economics had made itself unscientific by imitating physics, measuring what could be measured and ignoring the causes that could not.

Knowledge is dispersed

The knowledge that matters is not in papers; it is the detail of here and now — who is short-staffed, who has stock left, which road is blocked. That list exists in no single head, and no reporting line can assemble it before it expires.

Zhang Yiming's feed gathers scattered clicks into a signal: the price mechanism as a product. A head-office spreadsheet meant to replace the market is the counter-example.

A price is a protocol

A price is not a verdict on value; it is a compressed message. You do not need to know why it moved, only that it moved, in order to change what you do next.

When oil rises, drivers and chemical plants across the world change behaviour within days, and no minister had to call them together.

Spontaneous order

An order nobody designed is still an order. Language, markets and legal custom are all of this kind. You can intervene in them, but the cost of the intervention usually turns up somewhere you were not watching.

Taleb's antifragility says do not sand a system down until one person can control it. Hayek is the ancestor of that sentence.

The pretence of knowing

The dangerous thing is not ignorance; it is mistaking your local knowledge for the complete list. The planner's standard disease is the assumption that what he can see is all there is to see.

Annual plans in large companies tend to expire around April, not because the people are stupid, but because the ground keeps moving under the plan.

Rules beat commands

A good order constrains behaviour with rules rather than specifying results with orders. Give people a boundary and let them fill it in with the knowledge they have and you do not.

Shang Yang legislated instead of issuing daily orders to each magistrate; Dalio's principles are a boundary, not a trading manual.

How do I use it today?

Before you write the annual plan, ask what share of the knowledge it depends on is not in your head, and write those blanks down as a list. If the list runs past one page, stop writing the plan as a set of orders and rewrite it as boundaries and signals: what people may not do, and what tells them how much something matters this week.
