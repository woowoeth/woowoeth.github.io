Robert K. Merton

Self-confirming

Robert K. Merton (United States · 1910-2003) — Self-confirming.

How the world worksThe industrial ageSelf-confirming
An American sociologist, born in a poor quarter of Philadelphia in 1910 to immigrant parents, who taught at Columbia from 1941 until he retired. He never built a grand system. He did one thing: take a mechanism people could feel but could not name, give it an exact name, and then show how it runs. Several words now in ordinary use are his - self-fulfilling prophecy, role model, unintended consequences, the Matthew effect. They lasted because each one has a mechanism behind it that can be retold, not just a clever phrase. In 1994 he received the National Medal of Science, the first sociologist to do so.

What actually happened?

He was not born Merton. Meyer Robert Schkolnick arrived in South Philadelphia in 1910, the son of Eastern European Jewish immigrants. As a boy he earned money doing magic and took a stage name: Merlin, after the magician at Arthur's court. A friend told him it was a cliche, so he changed it to something that sounded English, Merton, and kept it for the rest of his life. Decades later the boy who had chosen his own name wrote the paper on how differently the world remembers the same contribution depending on whose name is attached.

His work was naming mechanisms

Self-fulfilling prophecy, role model, unintended consequences, the Matthew effect: all his. He picked things everyone could sense and nobody could name, because only a named thing can be pointed at, argued about and guarded against.

The thing that keeps happening on your team changes character the moment it finally has an exact name.

A false definition can produce a true result

In 1948 he set out the self-fulfilling prophecy: a wrong reading of a situation prompts behaviour that turns the situation into what was read. His case is the bank run - the rumour says it is failing, depositors withdraw, it fails.

Enough people call the project doomed, the strong ones transfer out, and the project is duly doomed.

Afterwards the prophet looks vindicated

The cruelty is in the aftermath. Whoever said it points to what happened as proof, and the thing happened precisely because they said it. His phrase for this state of affairs is a reign of error kept alive by a specious validity.

The person certain she was never up to it is often the person who stopped handing her anything.

Recognition is paid on reputation held

In 1968 he named a second mechanism in Science. Work by someone of standing draws extra recognition; identical work by an unknown has it withheld. The name comes from Matthew's gospel: to every one that hath shall be given.

The same memo is decisive under one signature and premature under another.

He was caught by his own finding

That paper stands on his student Zuckerman's interviews and carries only his name. Twenty years on he published the correction: it should have been joint. Nobody had to act badly for the effect to work, which is the strongest evidence for it.

When you last said the team did it, did people remember the team or the person saying it?

How do I use it today?

Take one thing you are being misread on, or misreading someone else on, and do two things. First, write the judgement out as a full sentence - he is not really trying, they are guarding themselves against me - then ask which pieces of your evidence existed before that sentence did. Usually none; the rest accumulated afterwards. Second, if you are the one being guarded against, stop working on the explanation. Look for an arrangement where neither side has to trust the other: shared numbers, shared access, a written scope. Explaining keeps you inside the loop.
