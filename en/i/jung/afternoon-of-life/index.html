You can't run the afternoon on the morning's programme

Not wrong. Expired

Jung, The Stages of Life, 1930–31

Something feels wrong around forty. Is it a fault? Jung said it's the hour.

Carl JungNot wrong. Expired

What actually happened?

Jung compared a life to a day. In the morning the task points outward — a career, a family, a place in the world — and the standards come from outside and genuinely work. His observation was that many people stall in midlife not because their capacity declined but because they are still running the afternoon on the morning's programme. His line is severe: what was great in the morning will be little at evening. Not that the programme was wrong. It expired.

Stalling is usually the hour, not a malfunction

Someone in their late thirties doing well and suddenly unable to summon anything looks first for a defect: tiredness, wrong industry. Jung offers another reading — the morning's task is largely done, the afternoon's hasn't been picked up, and the gap between them is the feeling.

Having everything you were meant to want and feeling emptier is harder to explain to anyone than having nothing.

The morning's fix fails twice as hard in the afternoon

Stuck in the morning, the answer is push harder and ask for more. Use that again in the afternoon and it only packs the empty place fuller. Plenty of people add in midlife — bigger house, higher title — and find the feeling entirely unchanged.

The promotion made it emptier, which says the thing being added was never on the ledger.

The afternoon's task points inward, not outward

Jung says the direction reverses: from acquiring to sorting, from proving outward to picking back up the parts of yourself that never got used. The judge changes too — the morning is scored by others, the afternoon only by you.

Picking up the thing you put down at twenty, mostly not in order to achieve anything with it.

How do I use it today?

Where you are: you're in your thirties or forties, doing reasonably well, and increasingly unable to summon anything, without being able to say what's wrong.

Ask first: is the effort I'm making still answering the morning's question? If that one is largely answered, adding more won't work.

Where it goes wrong: using it as a reason to abandon a career — Jung is describing a shift of weight, not walking off; and don't read every bout of tiredness as a turn in your life. Sometimes you're tired.
