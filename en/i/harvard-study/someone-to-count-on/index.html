Who can you call at 2am

The count doesn't matter, the catch does

A measure used throughout the study

The shortest way to measure your relationships. Why this question is so hard to fool.

What actually happened?

The study has long used one very short question to measure relationships: if you were sick or frightened at two in the morning, who could you call. It beats 'how many friends do you have' because it asks three things at once — whether the person exists, whether you believe they'd pick up, and whether you could bring yourself to dial. Miss any one and the answer is blank. Plenty of socially busy people have no name here; plenty of quiet ones name two or three immediately.

Three tests in one question, so it can't be gamed

Someone to call tests supply. Believing they'd answer tests quality. Being able to dial tests you. Other people supply the first two; only you can clear the third — and a lot of people stall there, carrying it alone rather than making the call.

You can think of the name, and then: 'it's too late to bother them.' That's the third test.

No name doesn't mean no one

This answer is found by trying, not by thinking. A relationship you've never asked anything of is one whose strength you don't know. And asking the first time is usually easier than imagined — most people feel trusted rather than imposed upon.

After the first real ask, the relationship usually gets closer, not more distant.

Turn it around: whose name are you

The question works in reverse. Does anyone think of you at two in the morning? Being someone's fallback runs both ways, and being permanently filed as 'lovely, but I wouldn't trouble them' is a shallow relationship. Letting people impose on you is its own kind of reliability.

Nobody ever asking you for help may not mean you're well-liked. It may mean nobody expects you to say yes.

How do I use it today?

Where you are: you want to know how solid your relationships actually are, and have no way to measure it.

Ask first: who could I call at 2am? Write the names down. If none come, don't panic — ask the follow-up: is it that there's no one, or that I couldn't make the call?

Where it goes wrong: using it to rank the people around you or tally what you owe; and a blank answer is not a verdict. It measures where you are now, not who you are.
