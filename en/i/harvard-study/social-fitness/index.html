Relationships need training, like a body

Untrained, they decline

Robert Waldinger's term, social fitness

Everyone knows a body declines without training. So do relationships. What social fitness actually asks you to do.

Harvard Study of Adult DevelopmentUntrained, they decline

What actually happened?

Waldinger attached a phrase to the study's conclusion: social fitness. Relationships behave like muscle — you don't finish them, and untrained they decline. Very few people in the study fell out with anyone. They moved once, changed jobs once, got busy for a few years, and by the time they looked up the numbers were still in the phone and the people were gone. The action he gives is small: pick one person and get in touch this week, not to arrange anything, just to say how things are.

It isn't whether you have them, it's whether it moved recently

Most people judge that they 'have friends' from their contact list and their memory. What does the work is the live part — the people you have actually exchanged something with in the last six months. Counted that way, the number is usually far smaller than assumed.

Two thousand contacts, fewer than ten spoken to in six months. That second number is the real size.

Small moves beat plans

'We should get together more' almost never happens, because it names no day and no person. What works is the smallest step: one message to one person about nothing in particular. The bar is low enough to survive a busy month, and going quiet is what relationships can't survive.

One person a week is fifty a year. Waiting until things calm down is usually zero.

Which gap to fill depends on which one is empty

The study separates relationships by what they do: someone to fall back on, someone to enjoy things with, someone to say the true thing to, someone whose judgement you want. They don't substitute. Find the empty slot first, then decide who.

A crowd to drink with and nobody to call at 2am isn't a shortage of socialising.

How do I use it today?

Where you are: you don't think of yourself as unpopular, but when you actually need someone you don't know who to call.

Ask first: who have I contacted in the last six months for no practical reason? Write the names. If you can't reach five, start there.

Where it goes wrong: turning it into a task with a streak — a relationship that becomes a metric is harder to keep; and it doesn't mean reviving everyone. You're training a few lines, not the whole address book.

Lines to keep

Nobody sets out to lose a friend. They get busy and it goes quiet.
