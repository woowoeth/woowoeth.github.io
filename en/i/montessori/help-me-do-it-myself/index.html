Help me to do it myself

Doing it for them saves your time

The Secret of Childhood

Doing it for them takes two minutes. What those two minutes bought, and what they cost.

Maria MontessoriDoing it for them saves your time

What actually happened?

Montessori compressed the whole method into a child's request: help me to do it myself. That draws the boundary of help — the goal is that they can finish alone, not that the task finishes sooner. What an adult saves by taking over is their own time; what is spent is that one chance to learn. She also described sensitive periods: stages when a child absorbs a particular capacity with almost no effort. Hit the window and it is nearly free. Miss it and the same thing costs several times as much.

Lower the difficulty to just within reach

The right intervention is neither taking over nor leaving them to it. It's adjusting the difficulty: break the task to a step they can do now and leave that step to them. It's the same thing deliberate practice calls the edge of the comfort zone, happening at three.

They spill the water every time. Give them a small container to practise the feel, rather than pouring it yourself from now on.

Sensitive periods: wrong timing doubles the work

At two, a child is intensely sensitive to order, and habits of putting things away form easily. Try to establish the same rule at seven and the difficulty is completely different. Reading which period they're in beats working from an age chart.

Suddenly everything must go back in its exact place. That's not stubbornness. It's the window.

Slow is a cost and an investment

Letting a child do it will be slower and messier. Accepting that cost requires a longer time horizon: the ten extra minutes today buys years of not having to be involved. When the arithmetic doesn't get done, adults take over indefinitely.

Dressing them yourself on a rushed morning is fine. If every morning is rushed, the arithmetic never happened.

How do I use it today?

Where you are: they're slow and doing it badly and you can't stop yourself taking over.

Ask first: am I taking over so they learn, or so this ends sooner? Could I just drop the difficulty one notch?

Where it goes wrong: turning 'let them do it' into no help at all. Repeated failure makes a child give up outright.

Lines to keep

Help me to do it myself.

Every unnecessary help is an obstacle to development.

Ten extra minutes today buys years of not being involved.
