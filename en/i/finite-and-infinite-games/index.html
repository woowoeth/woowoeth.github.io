Finite and Infinite Games

Win, or keep playing

Finite and Infinite Games (1986 · James P. Carse) — Win, or keep playing.

How the world worksModernWin, or keep playing
A slim book by a professor of religious history at New York University: 101 short sections, no argument, only statements. The distinction it offers is very simple — a finite game is played to win, an infinite game to continue the play. Once it is in your head it rearranges almost everything you thought about ambition, competition, careers and companies, because you start noticing how many of the games you are in were never required to have a winner at all.

What actually happened?

The book landed with almost no response. It was not a business book and not quite an academic one — a historian of religion writing in aphorisms about games, theatre and death, which pleased neither side. It was properly read only in the internet era, when founders found that it described precisely something they could not name: why some companies win every battle and die, while others never declare victory and are still there. Its best-known reader in China is Wang Xing, and the Meituan value about being patient for the long term stands on this book.

Two kinds of games

A finite game has a fixed start, end and winner, its rules cannot change, and players play inside the boundaries. An infinite game has no end, its rules must change so that the play can continue, and players play with the boundaries themselves.

A funding round, a sale, a quarter are finite. A company, a relationship, a person's craft are infinite. Play an infinite game as a finite one and on the day you win you will not know what to do.

The rules point in opposite directions

A finite game's rules cannot change or the game is over; an infinite game's rules must change or the game is over. This is the sharpest edge in the book: every 'we have always done it this way' is quietly demoting an infinite game to a finite one.

The policy that made the company work at thirty people, left untouched at three hundred, is not tradition. It is a countdown.

Power and strength

Finite players compete for power, which is respect attached to a title and is handed to you by other people. Infinite players play with strength, which needs nobody's recognition and cannot be taken away.

Whatever is still there after the title is swapped out is the strength. Most career anxiety comes from the two having been treated as one thing for years.

Serious against playful

Finite players are serious, because the outcome touches their title. Infinite players are playful, because they can change the rules at any moment. Playful does not mean careless; it means having kept the freedom to change something.

The person who can laugh in the middle of a negotiation usually has another option. Seriousness is often just the absence of one.

Titles need an audience

A finite game needs spectators to certify the win — a champion nobody saw does not hold. An infinite game has no audience, only players. Any achievement that has to be watched in order to exist is a finite game.

If the reason to do it evaporates the moment nobody will know, that was a finite game wearing a long-term costume.

How do I use it today?

Next time a situation has you tense, ask one question before any other: is this finite or infinite? If it is finite, define what winning means, play it out, and leave it at the office. If it is infinite, stop chasing an endgame and put your attention on staying able to keep playing. It works especially well on careers — a promotion is a finite game, capability is an infinite one, and only one of them ends.
