Kevin Kelly

True fans

Kevin Kelly (2008 · founding editor of Wired) — True fans.

Starting and buildingModernTrue fans
Founding editor of Wired. In 2008 he turned the survival problem of the independent creator into arithmetic: a thousand true fans, a hundred dollars each a year, is a hundred thousand dollars — enough for one person to live on. His definition of a true fan is strict: someone who will buy anything you produce and drive two hundred miles to see you play. The value of the sum is that it takes fame off the list of requirements. He attached a second condition most people forget: the money has to reach you directly.

What actually happened?

The fashionable idea in 2008 was the long tail — infinite shelf space, everything findable, an audience for every niche. Kelly's essay opens by granting the point and then turning it over. The long tail is famously good news for a handful of aggregators and for consumers; for the individual creator standing out in the tail it is a decidedly mixed blessing, because the tail is long and it is thin. So he asked a different question. Not how do I climb toward the head of the curve, but how many people, exactly, does one person need. Then he did the multiplication.

A thousand can be counted; a million is a lottery

The solid part of the sum is that a thousand people can be reached three a day for a year, which is a schedule somebody can actually execute. A million is not assembled by effort. Swap the target and the working day changes shape immediately.

Did one more person who will actually come back arrive today? You can ask that every evening. Did I go viral today? You cannot.

A hundred dollars a year is a design brief

Read the number backwards and it stops being a revenue target and becomes a question about what you make: what must exist in a year that somebody who genuinely likes you will spend a hundred dollars on? That pushes you off producing more and onto producing one thing worth buying.

A whole year of free updates is worth less than one thing they will pay for: a course, a printed book, an evening in a room.

Whoever holds the list holds your living

There is one test, and it is unkind. If this platform throttled or banned you tomorrow, could you still reach those thousand people? If not, you never had a thousand fans — you were renting traffic and calling it an audience.

A mailing list, a group chat, your own site: anything you could walk out with counts. A follower number does not.

The cut takes the feedback, not only the money

With an intermediary in the middle you cannot see who bought, why they bought, or when they stopped. The percentage is the visible loss; the invisible one is the information that would have told you what to change next.

One reply explaining why somebody unsubscribed is worth more than a retention curve on a dashboard.

How do I use it today?

If you make things and keep waiting for one to break out, count two numbers first: how many people would pay you today, and how many of them you can name. Then do the more urgent thing — before growing the audience further, solve how you would take them with you, by putting one direct route to yourself into every piece you publish. Do it in the other order and the day your follower count looks best is usually the day your leverage is lowest.
