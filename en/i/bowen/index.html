Differentiation

Murray Bowen (1978 · Family systems theory) — Differentiation.

Family and relationshipsModernDifferentiation
The psychiatrist who assembled family systems theory. His central concern is differentiation of self: whether a person can stay in contact with the people closest to him and still keep his own thinking and his own feeling. Poorly differentiated people are carried by the family's emotional field — somebody else is anxious, so they are anxious; a position gets given up to end a quarrel. His other major idea is the triangle: when two people carry more tension than they can hold, a third gets pulled in to spread it, and that fixes the tension in place.

What actually happened?

Between 1954 and 1959, on a research ward at the National Institute of Mental Health, he did something nobody else was doing: he moved whole families in to live. The identified patient was usually a young adult diagnosed with schizophrenia, and the point of having the mother, the father and the siblings on the ward was that you could then watch what everybody did, all day, for months at a time. What he saw was not one ill person among well ones. It was a system holding a shape, with the symptom sitting exactly where the system needed it to sit.

Connection and separateness at the same time

Low differentiation has two forms that look opposite and share a root: being led by the family's feeling, and cutting the family off. He calls the first fusion and the second emotional cut-off. Differentiation is the third thing — in the room, and the position holds.

Pressed about marriage over the new year: miserable all evening is fusion, never going back is cut-off. The third road is going as usual, without arguing and without changing your mind.

The triangle fixes the tension

Two people under strain instinctively reach for a third: complaining about a partner to a friend, parents recruiting a child into their quarrel. It relieves the day, and the cost is that the pair no longer has to settle anything, because the pressure now has somewhere else to go.

Rather than raise it with the colleague, you raise it with a third one. The problem does not go away; it becomes a permanent structure with three people in it.

Anxiety runs through the system

In a family, anxiety behaves like water: it runs from wherever the pressure is towards whoever is most vulnerable, which is very often a child. So a parent handling their own share does more than any technique aimed at the child. It is his most usable conclusion.

Your anxiety about his prospects reaches him whether or not a word is said. Deal with your part and his state often shifts on its own.

It is practised, and it is inherited

Your level is largely set in the family you grew up in and gets passed down, and it can be raised on purpose. The practice happens in the relationship most able to sweep you off, and it sounds like this: I know you are unhappy, I understand, and my view has not changed.

Meet a relative's feeling without going cold and without surrendering, and the capacity grows a notch. The next generation starts from that notch.

How do I use it today?

When your family's feeling is carrying you along, sort it first: am I fusing — going miserable, conceding fast — or cutting off? And when two people you know are stuck, notice whether you have become the third point. Handing the problem back is the only move that lets them solve it.
