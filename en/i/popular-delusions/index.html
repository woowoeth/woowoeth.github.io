Extraordinary Popular Delusions (Britain · 1841) — Manias.

Money and riskThe industrial ageManias
Charles Mackay's Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds, first published in 1841 and in print ever since. Its opening chapters are on money: John Law's Mississippi Scheme, the South Sea Bubble and the Dutch tulip mania. Many of its anecdotes are second-hand and it is no careful history, but it leaves a way of watching crowds. It is here because no entry yet says that buyers stop wanting the thing itself, or that nobody tells you when it is over.

What actually happened?

A wealthy Dutch merchant, proud of his rare tulips, rewarded a sailor who brought news of a cargo with a red herring for breakfast. The sailor, fond of onions, saw what looked like one on the counter, pocketed it and ate it with his herring on the quay. It was a Semper Augustus bulb worth three thousand florins. The household found him sitting on a coil of ropes, finishing the last of it. Mackay, who took the story from an older travel book, says that breakfast could have fed a whole ship's crew for a year.

One bulb, one list

Mackay copies out what was handed over for a single Viceroy bulb: two lasts of wheat, four of rye, four fat oxen, eight fat swine, twelve fat sheep, wine, beer, butter, a thousand pounds of cheese, a bed, a suit of clothes and a silver cup. Total: 2,500 florins.

Turn a price into things you can see and use. It often sobers you up at once.

From nobles to chimney-sweeps

At the height of it nobles, citizens, farmers, mechanics, seamen, footmen, maid-servants, even chimney-sweeps and old clothes-women dabbled in tulips. People turned their property into cash to buy flowers, and houses and land went at ruinously low prices.

When people who never touch this kind of thing start telling you about it, most of the buyers are already in.

No rescue and no court

After the crash, tulip-holders sent deputies to Amsterdam to ask the government for a remedy. It refused at first to interfere. In the end contracts made before November 1636 were declared void, later buyers were released on paying ten per cent, and the courts would not hear gambling debts.

Money staked on a mania is rarely bailed out when it goes wrong.

What part do we leave out?

We take only the chapters on money - the Mississippi Scheme, the South Sea Bubble and the tulipomania - and leave the ones on alchemists, prophecies, fortune-telling, the magnetisers, the Crusades and the witch mania. Many of its anecdotes are second-hand from older books and travellers' accounts, and later historians working in the Dutch archives think Mackay overstated the scale of the tulip episode; we read it for how crowds think, not as a record of what happened.

How do I use it today?

Next time you are about to follow everyone into something that is soaring, write one line first: am I buying it to use, or only to sell to the next person? If it is the second, write down who that person is.
