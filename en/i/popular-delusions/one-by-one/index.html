Extraordinary Popular Delusions

One by one

Nobody announces the end

Charles Mackay, Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds, Preface; and 'The South-Sea Bubble', the company for 'an undertaking of great advantage'

There is no signal to wake up. When everyone is pushing in, what lets you be the one who stops and asks?

Extraordinary Popular DelusionsNobody announces the end

What actually happened?

In 1720, the year of the South Sea Bubble, new companies were floated in London almost daily. The most absurd, Mackay says, was 'A company for carrying on an undertaking of great advantage, but nobody to know what it is'. The prospectus asked a deposit of two pounds a share and promised a hundred a year. At nine the next morning its founder opened an office in Cornhill and crowds besieged his door. By three o'clock a thousand shares were paid for. He left for the Continent that evening and was never heard of again.

The queue was all they knew

The company would not say what it did. All anyone in the queue could see was that plenty of people were ahead of them. Mackay's preface has the line for it: 'Men, it has been well said, think in herds; it will be seen that they go mad in herds'.

If you can't say how a venture makes money, only who else has invested, you are looking at the queue, not the venture.

Nobody wakes up together

The sentence goes on: 'while they only recover their senses slowly, and one by one.' Going mad has leaders and noise. Coming round has no signal, and nobody announces that it is over. By the time everyone wakes together, it has usually crashed.

Don't wait for someone in the group chat to say it's time to get out. By then the exit is crowded.

Ask what it is

The company put the answer in its name: nobody to know what it is. Anyone in that queue who asked what the business was would have got no answer, and for five hours nobody asked. Asking first, in a crowd, feels like spoiling things. That is why people wake one at a time.

Before you pay in, write three sentences on what it is and how it earns. If you can't, don't pay yet.

How do I use it today?

Where you are: suddenly everyone is talking about something and buying it. You can't quite say what it is, only that people you know are in.

Ask first: leaving aside that other people bought it, can I say in three sentences what it is and how it makes money?

Where it goes wrong: reading it as 'the crowd is always wrong, so do the opposite'. Mackay doesn't say crowds are always wrong; he says don't expect the crowd to come round for you. Doing the opposite is still steering by the crowd. The work is asking the question yourself.
