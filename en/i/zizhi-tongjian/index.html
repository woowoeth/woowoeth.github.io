Why regimes fall

The Comprehensive Mirror (Northern Song · 1065–1084) — Why regimes fall.

Power and organisationMedievalWhy regimes fall
Sima Guang directed the compilation for nineteen years: two hundred and ninety-four volumes covering the thirteen hundred and sixty-two years from 403 BC to AD 959. The emperor Shenzong named it — a mirror of what has been, useful to the art of governing. It is a history textbook written for a ruler, and it asks one question throughout: why do regimes rise and why do they fall. The Records of the Grand Historian is about people. This is about power as a system.

What actually happened?

The book was finished by a man who had lost the argument. Sima Guang opposed Wang Anshi's New Policies, lost, and left the capital for Luoyang, where he spent fifteen years out of office with nothing to do but this. The discarded drafts kept there are said to have filled two rooms, and a later reader who went through them reported that not one character had been written carelessly. He returned to power in 1085, the year after the book was presented, and was dead within about a year. The work that outlasted everything was the one he did while sidelined.

The consort clans and the eunuchs, again

One cycle recurs from the Later Han to the Tang: a child emperor, a dowager who brings in her own family, the family taking over, the emperor growing up and using the eunuchs against them, the eunuchs taking over, a new child emperor. Understand the loop and two dynasties become legible.

The modern version has different names. A vacuum in authority is filled by whoever is standing closest, every time.

What the hundred and eighty-six comments judge by

Sima Guang's own verdicts run on three things: the ritual order that fixes rank, government by character, and putting capable people in office. Reduced to one line, whoever holds the people holds the empire — offered as a finding from thirteen centuries, not as a sermon.

He is arguing that legitimacy is a resource with a balance that can be spent down, long before anyone put it that way.

The dangerous moment is the strong one

The pattern repeats: a capable ruler, hard work, prosperity, a peak — then the succession problem, the bad ministers, the consort clans, and collapse. He observed that nothing was ever destroyed from outside before it had rotted inside first.

The An Lushan rebellion arrived at the height of the Tang. The fall of the Northern Song came at its richest. Neither was struck down while weak.

Read it against the Records

The Records of the Grand Historian asks why a person succeeded or failed, and reads like a novel. This asks why a regime rose or fell, keeps to a timeline, and reads like a case file. One shows you how people work, the other how systems work.

On the same event, the Records gives you the last words at the river. This gives you how the strategic position had shifted over three years.

How do I use it today?

To judge whether an organisation is healthy, use its questions rather than its finances. Is there anybody who can check the person deciding? Does bad news travel upwards? Would anyone say out loud that the emperor has no clothes? The answers move before the numbers do.
