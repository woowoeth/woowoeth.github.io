Knowing where to stop

The two characters on his office wall

The scroll in his office, widely reported; Cheung Kong's operating principle

A businessman famous for expansion looks up at a scroll telling him to stop. Stopping and not daring aren't the same.

Li Ka-shingThe two characters on his office wall

What actually happened?

Li Ka-shing's office carries a scroll with two characters usually read as 'know where to stop', from a classical line: know where to stop and then you can be settled. Alongside it is the principle he repeats: never forget stability while growing, never forget growth while stable. In practice that meant selling down in overheated property, and walking away when a bid passed his line — moves others read as missing the market. He hung that scroll during Cheung Kong's most aggressive expansion. The faster you accelerate, the more you need a brake pad in permanent view.

Write the stop before you sit down at the table

In practice this means writing the exit condition before every entry: above this price we don't chase, past this amount we don't add, at this date we don't renew. Written while unemotional, it needs no heroism to execute. Recalculating 'is it worth it' in the room is calculating your pride.

Write your maximum before the auction and give it to a colleague. Every extra pound buys the feeling of not losing, not the asset.

What stops is the exposure, not the ambition

This gets misread as contentment with a little. His record says the opposite — decades from plastics to property to ports to telecoms, the ambition never paused. What was capped was the exposure of any single bet. Aggressive on direction, limited per bet: the second is what keeps the first alive.

Long-term ambition about the field, a hard ceiling on each round. Companies that cross several cycles usually run that combination.

Hang the brake pad where you see it daily

Framing those two characters on the office wall is mechanism design. This is the string that goes first when things are going well, so the reminder has to be physically present — hit once a day when you look up. A principle that lives only in the annual speech is gone by the expansion season.

Print the per-project ceiling and the gearing red line in the header of the decision-meeting template.

How do I use it today?

Where you are: the market is hot, the team is confident, and you're considering adding more.

Ask first: has the exit condition for this one been written? When — before entering, or just now?

Where it goes wrong: using 'know where to stop' as cover for never acting; or writing the line and then stepping over it because this time is special.
