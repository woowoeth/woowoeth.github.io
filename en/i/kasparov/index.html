Garry Kasparov

After losing to the machine

Garry Kasparov (1963– · Baku) — After losing to the machine.

Learning and growthModernAfter losing to the machine
World chess champion for fifteen years, from 1985 to 2000. In 1996 he beat IBM's Deep Blue in Philadelphia; in the May 1997 rematch in New York he lost 2.5–3.5, the first time a reigning world champion lost a match to a machine. Most people in this library are dealing with other people. He is dealing with something else: what is left of a craft you spent a life on after a machine passes you at the thing it is built on.

What actually happened?

The 1996 match is usually left out, and it matters. He won it 4–2, losing only the first game — the first time a machine had beaten a world champion under tournament conditions, which at the time was itself the headline. He then had a year to prepare, and IBM had a year to rebuild. What he faced in 1997 was not the machine he had beaten. That is the ordinary shape of it: you win the first round against a tool, conclude the threat was overstated, and meet a different thing the following year.

Strike out what is already lost

The day machines won on depth of calculation, that track closed. Training on a closed track has no exit however hard you train. The first move isn't redoubling effort; it's removing the lost item from your own list of what you are worth.

No amount of practice makes you faster than the model at drafting a first version. The real question is which part of the work that leaves you.

Change the rules, not the effort

He didn't train harder and he didn't leave chess. A year later he staged a tournament where each side brought a computer to the board. When a tool matches an ability, the valuable position moves to how the tool is used — and someone has to define that position first.

The translators who priced per word lost. The ones who charged for deciding what not to translate did not.

The appeal and the next move cost the same hours

He spent years arguing the match hadn't been fair, and withdrew all of it in 2017. Proving the loss didn't count and working out what to train next come out of the same person in the same stretch of time.

The months spent proving the review was unfair were the same months available for the work that would have made the review irrelevant.

How do I use it today?

When a core skill in your field gets matched by a tool, don't train harder first. Ask which item is now clearly lost, strike it out, and see what's left that the tool can't replace. And if you notice yourself repeating that it isn't actually any good, count how many times this month — then count what new thing you practised in the same month.
