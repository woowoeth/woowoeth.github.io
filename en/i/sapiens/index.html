Sapiens (2011 · Yuval Noah Harari) — Fictions.

How the world worksModernFictions
An Israeli historian's account of sixty thousand years, with an argument short enough to hold in one hand. Sapiens run the planet not because we are the strongest or the cleverest animal, but because we are the only one that can believe in things that are not there. Money, nations, laws, gods, limited companies: all of them are shared fictions, and shared fictions are what let millions of strangers act as one. The same book then turns the agricultural revolution inside out and argues that the species won while almost everyone in it lost.

What actually happened?

He sets two documents side by side. The Code of Hammurabi, from about 1776 BC, states that people fall into superiors, commoners and slaves, and that the gods arranged it that way. The American Declaration of Independence, from 1776 AD, states that all men are created equal. Both claim to be describing reality. Both claim to hold everywhere and forever. Both are, in his terms, imagined orders held up by belief rather than by anything you could measure. That comparison is the whole book in one page, and it cuts in both directions.

Fiction is the cooperation technology

Chimpanzees can coordinate a few dozen individuals, because coordination beyond that needs personal acquaintance. A shared story removes the acquaintance requirement. Two strangers who accept the same currency, the same law or the same brand can transact without ever meeting, and that is the only reason a million-person organisation is possible at all.

A national border is a line nobody can see, enforced because enough people on both sides agree it is there.

An imagined order is not a lie

Harari is careful about this. A lie is something the teller knows to be false. An imagined order is believed by everyone inside it, including the people it disadvantages, which is exactly why it holds. Calling it fiction says nothing about whether it is good, only about where it lives.

Human rights cannot be found in a body or a rock. They still stop wars, because enough people act as though they were physical facts.

The farmer worked more and ate worse

The standard story has farming as progress. Harari counts differently: longer hours, a narrower diet, new diseases, and a life more exposed to one bad harvest. What grew was the number of people, not the quality of any one life. He calls it history's biggest fraud.

Wheat covers more of the earth than any forager could have imagined. Judged by copies of DNA it is the winner of the story.

Capability rose; contentment did not follow

The last section of the book asks the awkward question. Modern people have more than any ancestor and are not reliably happier. If every measure of capability is up and subjective wellbeing is flat, then capability was never the thing being measured.

Ask what the last decade of improvements actually changed in an ordinary day of yours. Some of them did nothing at all.

How do I use it today?

Take one rule at work you have never questioned and ask where it actually lives. If it exists only because enough people act as though it does, then it is maintained by belief, and the question stops being is it right and becomes what keeps it believed. That is a question you can act on.
