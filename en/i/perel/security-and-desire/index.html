Security and desire want opposite things

Which is why the better it gets, the flatter

Esther Perel, Mating in Captivity, 2006

The steadier life gets, the less you feel, and most people assume they changed. Perel says the structure did it.

Esther PerelWhich is why the better it gets, the flatter

What actually happened?

Perel has done couples therapy for over thirty years, and the sentence she hears most is: everything is fine, we just don't feel anything. Her explanation blames nobody. Intimacy wants closeness, familiarity and predictability; desire wants distance, the unknown, the thing not yet fully held. Both are real needs and they point in opposite directions. So the more settled a relationship, the more easily the second gets squeezed out — not love going wrong, but one thing perfected until it crowds out the other.

It isn't love fading. Two needs are crowding each other

Read as love fading, the only available moves are blaming yourself or blaming them. Read as two needs crowding, the move is clear: not repairing the love, but making room again for the one that got squeezed out.

Ten years together, everything fine, nothing there. Not one word of that sentence says the love went wrong.

Familiar to the point of no unknown leaves nothing to miss

Desire needs a little opacity. When their schedule, thoughts and reactions are all inside your predictions, missing them has nowhere to land. So the problem often isn't too little communication — it's everything being accounted for.

Both schedules known to the hour is often the first bit of air a long relationship loses.

Room comes from separateness, not more closeness

The instinct is more time together, more talking. Perel's prescription often runs the other way: each go and do something the other isn't part of, become two people again, then come back. Distance isn't the enemy of love. It's the fuel of desire.

A few days apart and then meeting: that touch of strangeness is useful, not a warning sign.

How do I use it today?

Where you are: no fights, no crisis, and no feeling, and you can't say what's wrong.

Ask first: when did we last each do something separately and come back with something to say? If you can't recall, start there.

Where it goes wrong: using it to justify neglect — making room isn't withdrawing; and it doesn't mean falling in love again from scratch. This is about clearing space, not applying more force.

Lines to keep

'Everything's fine, we feel nothing' says nothing about love failing.
