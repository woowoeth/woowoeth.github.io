The early template

Attachment Theory (1950s–1970s · Bowlby and Ainsworth) — The early template.

Family and relationshipsModernThe early template
Bowlby worked with children who had lost their families in the war and argued that the pull towards closeness is an instinct, not a dependency to be outgrown. His collaborator Mary Ainsworth built the Strange Situation, separating one-year-olds from their caregiver and bringing them back, and read stable patterns out of the reunion rather than the parting. Those patterns become a person's default template for closeness ever afterwards — not a determinism, but the factory setting. Bowlby's own name for it is the internal working model.

What actually happened?

In 1952 James Robertson, working with Bowlby, took a camera into a London hospital and filmed a two-year-old through eight days of a minor admission. Parents were then allowed in for an hour a week, or not at all, and the wards reported that children settled quickly. He filmed at fixed times each day, with a clock in shot, so that nobody could say he had waited around for tears. What the film shows is not settling. It is protest, then despair, then a terrible calm the staff had been reading as adjustment. The room it was first shown to was hostile. The visiting rules changed anyway.

Watch the reunion, not the separation

The least intuitive part of the experiment: the child who does not cry is not the child who is not afraid. Avoidant infants showed arousal as high as the criers and had simply learned not to show it, because showing it had not worked.

The person on the team who never raises a problem may not have none. Look at how his last request for help ended; it tells you more than asking him now.

A secure base is what makes risk possible

Bowlby's core finding is that exploration runs on security: a child goes far out only while sure the mother is still there. Grown up, it is the same circuit. Support is not doing the thing for someone, it is their knowing that coming back is still possible.

If you want someone to make the call, say plainly that a wrong one gets cleared up together. That sentence moves more than any incentive.

A template, not a verdict

The patterns are stable and rewritable: a later steady relationship, therapy, one long friendship all change them. As an explanation the idea is worth a lot, as an excuse it does harm. It accounts for the starting position, not for where you finish.

Noticing that you go silent in a conflict is progress. I am just avoidant, nothing to be done, is a tool turned into a shield.

Two patterns lock together

The anxious partner pursues, the avoidant one withdraws, and each move produces the other: the closer she comes the further he goes, and the further he goes the more anxious she gets. Naming the loop converts you have a problem into we are stuck in a circuit, which is the version with a way out.

One presses for an answer, the other goes quiet. That is not a question of who is right; it is two coping styles triggering each other.

How do I use it today?

To judge whether somebody in a relationship feels safe, do not watch how calm they look — watch whether they ask for help when something goes wrong, and how the last such request was answered. And if you want someone to take a risk, give them somewhere to come back to before you give them the encouragement.
