Zhuge Liang

The Longzhong plan

Zhuge Liang (Three Kingdoms · 181–234) — The Longzhong plan.

Power and organisationQin, Han and the Six DynastiesThe Longzhong plan
The most admired administrator in Chinese history, and the standard image of both strategic foresight and working yourself to death. He described the three-way division of the empire before he had left his farm, built the state of Shu with Liu Bei, governed it for twenty years with a legal system nobody accused of favouritism, and went north six times against a much larger enemy because waiting was the worse option.

What actually happened?

On the last campaign, held on the Wuzhang Plains, Sima Yi refused battle and questioned the Shu envoy about his opposite number's daily habits instead. The envoy said the chancellor rose early and went to bed late, personally reviewed every punishment above twenty strokes, and ate very little. Sima Yi turned to his staff: eating little and handling much, how can he last? He was right, and within months he was proved right. The campaign was settled by which man would live longer, not by which had more ability.

A framework before there was anything to frame

Still on the farm, he sorted the board: Cao Cao not to be contended with, Sun Quan to be allied and not attacked, Jing province to be taken, Yi province to be the base. Then three moves in order, and a condition that had to appear before the last one.

A plan that names what it will not touch, and what has to become true before the big move, survives contact. A list of ambitions does not.

Attack because waiting is worse

If we do not attack, the royal cause dies anyway; better than sitting and waiting to die. Shu was the weaker state and time was working for Wei, so passivity was not the safe option, it was the slow version of the same defeat.

When the gap with a larger competitor widens every quarter you do nothing, holding position is a decision with a cost, not the absence of one.

The law that reached his favourite

Ma Su lost Jieting and was executed, though he was the man Zhuge Liang had promoted over everyone's advice and was personally close to. Then the second half: a memorial asking for his own demotion by three ranks. A system is credible at the point where it touches the favourite.

Everyone learns what the rules are from the first case involving someone the boss likes. Nothing written down changes that lesson.

The cost of doing all of it yourself

Every punishment above twenty strokes reviewed personally. He set a standard nobody else met, so he could not delegate, so nothing survived him. Being irreplaceable is a personal distinction and an organisational disaster.

If you cannot name the person who could run this without you, the thing you have built has an expiry date and it is your own.

How do I use it today?

If you find that everything is passing through you, ask which of the two it is: nobody has reached the standard, or nobody was ever actually taught it. Then take one thing this week and teach the standard instead of applying it. Producing your own replacement is the job.
