The four horsemen

Four signals that predict a breakup

He watches fifteen minutes of one argument. This piece is about what he is actually watching.

John GottmanFour signals that predict a breakup

What actually happened?

Gottman recorded thousands of couples talking, coded expression, tone and heart rate frame by frame, then tracked who divorced years later. What predicted a breakup wasn't how fiercely they fought but whether four signals appeared, which he called the four horsemen: criticism (attacking the person, not the act), contempt (sarcasm, eye-rolling), defensiveness (returning the blame) and stonewalling (shutting the other out). The single strongest predictor of divorce is contempt — and the research found it affects the recipient's immune function.

Criticism and complaint are different things

A complaint targets behaviour: you didn't do the washing up and I'm exhausted. Criticism targets character: you're just lazy. The first can be solved; the second can only be defended. Many people think they're raising an issue while every sentence defines the other person, and so the same problem runs ten years.

Swap 'you never deal with the kids' for 'I did all three pickups this week'. Same event, and the second can be caught.

Contempt is a slow poison, not venting

Eye-rolling, mockery, mimicking their tone — all of it says I am above you. It differs from anger: anger is about the event, contempt says the person is unworthy. It's the strongest single predictor in his data because it destroys the foundation, not one conversation.

'What would you know' and 'fine, you're always right' need correcting on the spot. That isn't arguing, it's demolition.

Defensiveness returns the ball, it doesn't clarify

'I've been busy, and so have you' looks like explaining and is a counter-attack. Its function is to refuse any share of responsibility, and once both sides are defending, the conversation becomes mutual evidence and never reaches a solution.

When the pickups come up, take the part you can admit — 'you did do all three' — before explaining your side.

How do I use it today?

Where you are: the same thing gets fought about and every round escalates.

Ask first: was that last sentence about the event or about the person? Any sarcasm or eye-rolling? Am I explaining or returning the ball?

Where it goes wrong: using the four horsemen to charge the other person — 'that's contempt' is itself an attack. It's a checklist for yourself, not a verdict.

Lines to keep

The more often the four appear, the likelier the breakup.
