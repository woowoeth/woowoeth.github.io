Weigh the opinion, do not count it

Neither a vote nor a verdict

Principles, the work principles

He rejects the boss deciding and he rejects one person one vote. What replaces them is a weighting, and it needs a record to run on.

Ray DalioNeither a vote nor a verdict

What actually happened?

Two familiar ways to decide badly: autocracy, where the boss rules and information cannot get in, and democracy, where one vote each lets ten amateurs outweigh one expert. Bridgewater runs a third. Everyone gets to speak, and opinions are weighted by the speaker's track record in that particular field. Someone who has called currencies right three times carries more weight on currencies. The foundation is the taping and the public scoring, because without a record a track record cannot be checked. The aim is that the best idea wins whoever it comes from.

Equal airtime, unequal weight

Letting everyone speak and letting every opinion count the same are different policies. The first lets information in. The second lets ten hunches outvote one informed judgement. Splitting them is the whole design: speaking is universal, weight is earned inside a field.

An intern should be able to challenge the technical call, and the two objections should not weigh the same. On what young users like, the weighting may invert.

Weight hangs on records, or it hangs on rank

With no record of who judged what and how it turned out, believability quietly becomes a synonym for seniority. That is why the transparency is not a cultural gesture. It is the data layer the weighting runs on, and without it the system reverts to hierarchy.

Start logging big calls in three columns: prediction, reasoning, outcome. Six months on, the loudest voice and the most accurate one are rarely the same person.

Weight resets at the edge of the field

Believability is domain-bound. An investing record does not confer weight on a hiring decision, and technical authority does not confer weight on pricing. The usual failure is halo spill, where the person who is excellent at one thing carries the most weight in every meeting.

A founder's word on the product is backed by a record. On legal exposure and pay bands, his instinct should queue with everyone else's.

How do I use it today?

Where you are: an important disagreement just got settled by whoever outranked the room.

Ask first: in this specific field, whose track record can actually be looked up? Are we weighing evidence or status?

Where it goes wrong: using believability to freeze the old guard in place so newcomers never build a first record, or invoking the weighting with no log to compute it from.
