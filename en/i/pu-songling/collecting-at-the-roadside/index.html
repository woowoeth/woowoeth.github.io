Collecting stories by the road

Using the hours nobody wants

The tea-stall account, told by later writers rather than by him

Forty years of a tutor's scattered hours. What he did with them, and how the material accumulated.

Pu SonglingUsing the hours nobody wants

What actually happened?

There is a persistent account that Pu Songling set out tea by the roadside and asked passers-by for strange stories, waiving the price of a bowl for each one, then wrote them up at home. It comes from later writers, not from him, and may not be literal. But it matches a checkable fact: he spent forty years as a live-in tutor in gentry households, with a great deal of scattered time around the teaching, and the nearly five hundred pieces accumulated across exactly those years. He didn't quit the job to write. He wrote on the job.

Accumulation needs somewhere fixed for things to land

Scattered hours turn into something not because there are many of them but because there is one fixed place that catches them. A thing overheard, a sentence that occurs to you — all into the same notebook. Without that catch, the same forty years simply disperse.

Every loose idea into one file beats the same ideas spread across ten places.

The material is local. You don't go far for it

His sources were the ordinary talk of villagers in the county he tutored in, not the fruit of travels through famous mountains. What's genuinely scarce isn't remarkable experience. It's the eye that treats what's around you as material — most people have as much of it and aren't collecting.

The complaints, gossip and small events you hear daily. A year separates the person who writes them down from the one who doesn't.

Not quitting is what let it run so long

The tutoring fed him and gave him forty uninterrupted years. Had he staked everything on writing, he probably couldn't have lasted. What kills a side project isn't too little investment. It's gambling your living alongside it and being forced to stop.

Keeping the job is what let the thing reach its tenth year.

How do I use it today?

Where you are: you want to do something long-term, you have only scraps of time, and you can't afford to quit.

Ask first: do I have one fixed place that catches whatever comes up? Build that before asking whether there's enough time.

Where it goes wrong: using him to argue against focus — he collected one kind of thing for forty years; or treating 'don't quit' as a reason to delay. What matters is that the catch gets used daily.
