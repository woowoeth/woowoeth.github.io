La Rochefoucauld

Virtues in disguise

La Rochefoucauld (France · 1613–1680) — Virtues in disguise.

Reading peopleEarly modernVirtues in disguise
A French duke and a loser of the Fronde. He spent half his life inside aristocratic conspiracies and came out of them with his political career finished and the sight of one eye damaged. In retirement he wrote the Maxims, taking generosity, loyalty, modesty and pity apart one at a time and finding the same thing sitting underneath each of them: self-love. It is not misanthropy. It is a man who saw that world from the inside refusing to keep lying to himself about it.

What actually happened?

At the battle of the Faubourg Saint-Antoine in 1652 a musket shot hit him in the face and blinded him for a time. He had already backed the wrong side in several conspiracies; the family lands were cut, the political life over. When his sight came back he did not return to court, he went to the salons instead. The interesting part is what he dissected first. The earliest manuscript of the Maxims carries a long essay on self-love, which he says changes like a chameleon and lives in everything and in nothing. Every aristocratic pretence in the book is one he had spent twenty years performing himself, which is why it reads as a confession and not an indictment.

Self-love is the floor under every motive

Self-love is the greatest of flatterers. It reports back to you continuously on what kind of person you are, and the report is always favourable. What you take to be your principles, your taste and your judgement are frequently just the version self-love picked out for you.

In a review, change I did it for the team to I wanted to be seen as someone who did it for the team. A surprising number of decisions look different immediately.

Virtues are vices in good clothes

Our virtues are most frequently but vices disguised. Generosity may be ambition spending money; modesty may be a longer route to praise; tolerance may be an unwillingness to be bothered. He is not saying virtue does not exist. He is saying do not infer motive from behaviour.

A consistently generous person tells you only that generosity pays here. Change the conditions and the direction of what pays changes. Read the structure, not the character.

Nobody complains about their judgement

Everyone complains of his memory, no one complains of his judgment. A weak memory is a hardware fault and costs nothing to admit. Weak judgement would mean every past decision rested on nothing, and nobody can hold that thought, so memory quietly rewrites the misjudgements instead.

Write one page before a big decision saying what you expect and why. Read it half a year later. The first time is usually a shock.

Hypocrisy tells you the exchange rate

Hypocrisy is the homage vice pays to virtue. Vice has to wear virtue's clothes to circulate, which proves virtue is the currency in that place, because nobody bothers disguising themselves as something worthless. So watch the direction of the pretending.

To find out what an organisation truly rewards, ignore the values on the wall and watch what a new hire has learned to perform by month three.

How do I use it today?

Two uses. Inward: when one of your own justifications comes together a little too smoothly, stop and ask whether the reason happens to be extremely comfortable. Outward: judge a partner not by the motive they state but by whether the thing pays them, and by whether it would still pay them after the conditions change. Verify goodwill as a structure rather than believing it as a quality.

Read alongside

The Art of Worldly WisdomTwo books of maxims with different jobs: Gracián teaches you how to get through this world, La Rochefoucauld only undertakes to stop you deceiving yourself. One supplies method, the other removes illusion. Benjamin FranklinFranklin believed virtue could be drilled in with a chart. La Rochefoucauld thought what gets drilled in is the appearance of virtue. Read the two together for a year. The AnalectsConfucius holds that self-cultivation leads to benevolence. La Rochefoucauld suspects self-cultivation is only the most sophisticated form self-love has found so far.
