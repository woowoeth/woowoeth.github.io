Praise the process, not the talent

Praising cleverness makes people avoid challenge

Mindset; the praise experiments

One sentence of praise changes what a child picks next. This piece is about where the change happens.

Carol DweckPraising cleverness makes people avoid challenge

What actually happened?

After a set of problems she gave students one of two kinds of praise: one group heard 'you're so clever', the other 'you worked hard'. Offered a choice of the next set, the ones praised for cleverness took the easier one more often, the ones praised for effort took the harder one. Her reading: praise for talent makes people avoid challenge; praise for process makes them accept it. Talent praise turns results into proof of talent, so every later challenge becomes a threat to the self. The watershed is the moment of failure — information in one mindset, verdict in the other.

Praise the specific move, not the label

'You're clever' is a label, and labels need protecting. 'You went back through every wrong answer' is a move, and moves can be repeated. The test is simple: can they do this praise again next time?

They did well: ask how they prepared. Don't say they've got a good brain. The first is repeatable, the second can only be defended.

The moment of failure decides the direction

In good weather the two mindsets look identical. The watershed is the instant something fails: one reads it as 'I can't', the other as 'that approach can't'. The difference isn't the intensity of the feeling. It's whether they go looking for the cause.

A rejected proposal: the person who asks 'which part doesn't hold' fixes it. The one who decides they're not suited never proposes again.

The word 'yet'

Replacing 'failed' with 'not yet passed' turns a result into a point in time. The small change works because it moves attention from what level I am to where I've got to.

Change 'I can't write' to 'I can't write yet'. One word decides whether the next sentence looks for a method or an excuse.

How do I use it today?

Where you are: giving feedback to a child or a report.

Ask first: is this praising talent or a specific move? Could they do it again next time?

Where it goes wrong: turning process praise into a mechanical formula and never mentioning a genuinely good result — that reads as being fobbed off.

Lines to keep

Use 'not yet' instead of 'can't'.
