Self-compassion is not self-esteem

One of them needs a comparison and the other does not

Self-Compassion; the Self-Compassion Scale and its reviews

Self-esteem is the word this generation grew up inside. The question is why it goes missing exactly when it is needed.

Kristin NeffOne of them needs a comparison and the other does not

What actually happened?

Self-esteem runs on an algorithm: I am better than others. It draws its power from comparison, so it is at full strength when things go well and is first to leave when you fail. The moment you need it most is the moment it is not there. What she puts in its place does not compare: self-compassion asks not am I good enough, but what do I need now that this hurts. Across studies it does better on most measures — lower depression and anxiety, more motivation to correct mistakes — without the fragility of having to keep winning.

Comparison is the fuel, so it fails in the trough

As long as better than others sits inside the definition, supply depends on continuing to win. One failure and the supply is cut, which is precisely the moment support was required. Self-compassion does not depend on rank, so it is still present in the trough.

Somebody from your own year overtakes you. Self-esteem says I am finished. Self-compassion says this hurts, that is normal, what is the next move.

It does not lower the bar, it raises the will to fix things

The standing objection is that being kind to yourself makes you slack. The data point the other way: moving attention off am I a failure and onto what do I do about this makes people readier to admit errors and quicker to start repairing. Shame makes people hide.

An evening spent insulting yourself over a bad result means not opening the paper next day. Change the sentence and you can go through the wrong answers that night.

The usable test is whether you would say it to a friend

This is the most operable thing in her work. Take the sentence you are saying to yourself and say it, word for word, to a friend in the identical position. If it will not come out of your mouth, the sentence is faulty. It is a calibration, not an exercise in leniency.

You are too stupid to be doing this — you would not say it to a friend, so it does not get said to you either.

How do I use it today?

Where you are: you made a mess of something, the abuse in your head is getting louder and you are getting less able to move.

Ask first: could I say this sentence, unchanged, to a friend in the same position? If it will not come out, it is not the truth, it is an attack.

Where it goes wrong: taking it as permission to stop reviewing what happened — it asks you to move attention from who I am back to what to do; or using it to chase feeling good, which is self-esteem again.
