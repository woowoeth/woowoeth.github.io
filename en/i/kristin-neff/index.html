Self-compassion

Kristin Neff (2003 · University of Texas) — Self-compassion.

Mind and feelingModernSelf-compassion
The psychologist who in 2003 turned self-compassion from a devotional word into a measurable psychological variable; the scale she built has been used across a large body of research since. Her contribution is not advice to be nicer to yourself. It is an alternative algorithm to self-esteem. Self-esteem has better than others inside its definition, so it runs on comparison: strongest when things go well, first to leave when you fail. Self-compassion does not compare. It asks only what do I need now that this hurts. (Effect sizes in this field are disputed and the scales are self-report; this entry takes her structure, not the coefficients.)

What actually happened?

She has described where it started. After her son was diagnosed with autism, she found that everything which had taught her to be strong was useless, because all of it required her to prove first that she could take it. What let her keep going was permission to say that this was hard and that she was suffering, without adding the clause and therefore I am not up to it. That distinction — between admitting the difficulty and convicting yourself of inadequacy — is the whole of the research programme that followed, in one moment.

Self-esteem runs on comparison, so it fails in the trough

While better than others sits inside the definition, the thing has to keep winning to keep supplying. One failure cuts the supply, and that is precisely the moment support was needed. Self-compassion is not indexed to rank, so it is present in the trough. This is not a quarrel about attitude, it is two different power sources.

Overtaken by somebody from your own year, self-esteem says I am finished. Self-compassion says this hurts, that is normal, what is the next move.

It does not lower the standard, it raises the will to correct

The standing objection is that kindness to yourself makes you lazy. The data run the other way: moving attention from am I a failure to what do I do about this makes people readier to own the error and quicker to start repairing it. Shame makes people hide; self-compassion makes them look again.

An evening of self-abuse after a bad result means not opening the paper the next day. Change the sentence and the wrong answers get reviewed that night.

Three parts, and all of them have to be present

Self-kindness, common humanity, mindfulness. The value of the model is diagnostic rather than motivational: when it hurts, check the three and usually exactly one is missing. Most people are missing common humanity, the sense that others have been here. A smaller number are missing mindfulness, and are either replaying it or insisting they are fine.

Clear-eyed about the situation, aware other people go through it, and still refusing to be civil to yourself: the missing part is the first one.

The best test is whether you would say it to a friend

Take the sentence you are saying to yourself and say it word for word to a friend in the identical position. If it will not come out of your mouth, then it is not the truth, it is an attack. The test needs no theory to run and is hard to cheat.

You are too stupid to be attempting this. You would not say it to a friend, so it does not get said to you either.

How do I use it today?

After you make a mess of something, run one calibration first: try saying the sentence in your head, word for word, to a friend in the same position, and replace it if it will not come out. Then check the three parts. Am I willing to be civil to myself? Do I think this only happens to me? Have I weighed how serious it actually is, neither inflated nor denied? Usually only one is missing. Used wrongly: as a permit to stop reviewing what happened, or turning everyone goes through this into so stop complaining.
